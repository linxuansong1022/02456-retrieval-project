Training DPR on FiQA
two runs, step by step

I trained the dual-encoder retriever from our repo on FiQA on my laptop GPU, twice. The first run (BERT-base, three epochs, in-batch negatives only) ended below both lexical baselines; sections 01 to 06 and 09 go through it step by step. Section 07 is why I think it lost, and section 08 is the second run: ModernBERT, BM25 hard negatives, a batch of 128 and a much smaller temperature, scored against TF-IDF and BM25 with the same code.

01

The task

Given a finance question, rank the passages so that the ones a person actually gave as answers come first.

FiQA is a set of personal finance questions from forum threads, each paired with the answers that were posted to it. We load it from the Hugging Face copy vibrantlabsai/fiqa. The search corpus in our code is the union of every gold answer across the three splits, so each of the passages is the right answer to some question. The model has to tell answers apart from other answers, which is harder than separating answers from random text.

Why dense retrieval here: questions are short and written by people asking for help, while the answers are long and written by people who know the vocabulary. BM25 can only score words the two texts share. A trained encoder can learn that different phrasings belong together. Whether that works with 14k training pairs and a 6 GB card is what this run was meant to find out.

02

The model

Two BERT towers, one for questions and one for passages, trained so that a question lands close to its own answers.

The loss, with in-batch negatives

A batch holds B = 16 (question, gold passage) pairs. Every question is scored against all 16 passages in the batch, which gives a 16 by 16 score matrix. The diagonal holds the pairs that belong together; the other 15 passages in each row are someone else's answer and act as negatives for free. The loss is cross-entropy over each row, the InfoNCE form the DPR paper uses:

Li = −log exp(s(qi, pi) / τ)Σj=1..B exp(s(qi, pj) / τ)   B = 16, τ = 1

One detail matters for reading the loss curve below. Our loss L2-normalises both vectors before the dot product, so every score is a cosine in [−1, 1], and the temperature is 1. That caps how confident the model can be. Even a perfect model, with cosine 1 to its own passage and −1 to all 15 others, still pays

Lmin = log(1 + 15·e−2) =   perfect model
Luniform = log 16 =   model that scores every passage the same

In practice the loss lives between those two lines. A smaller τ would widen the band. The paper uses the raw dot product without normalising, which has no such floor.

03

Training loss

Every optimiser step of the run, read back out of the progress bar in train.log.

What I read from the curve

The loss drops quickly through the warm-up and the first part of epoch 1, then flattens out and creeps down for the remaining two epochs. It never gets anywhere near the floor. My best guess is that the temperature is the main problem, not the number of epochs. With cosine scores and τ = 1 every logit sits in [−1, 1], so the softmax over 16 passages can never get sharp: even the perfect model from section 02 only puts e / (e + 15/e) ≈ 33% of the probability on the right passage. Every pair keeps getting a large gradient, whether it is already ranked first or not, and the hard cases do not stand out.

That was a guess when I wrote it. Section 08 tests it: a short τ check first, then a second run that also adds BM25 hard negatives, a bigger batch and a newer backbone.

04

Validation after each epoch

After each epoch the trainer encodes all passages, retrieves for the 500 validation questions and scores the ranking with the repo's metrics.

How the repo computes recall

calculateRecall divides the hits in the top k by min(k, number of gold passages), not by the number of gold passages. At k = 1 this makes recall the same as precision@1, and for a question with three gold answers recall@5 only needs three of them in the top 5 to reach 1.0. It is a fair way to compare methods on the same data, but it is not the textbook recall and it is not the paper's metric either. Precision and MRR are cut at k too.

Against the paper

The number people quote from the DPR paper is top-20 accuracy on Natural Questions: the share of questions where at least one of the top 20 passages contains the answer string. Our recall is a different quantity, so for this row I also counted the share of test questions with at least one labelled gold passage in the top k (hit@k). That is the closest thing we have to the paper's definition.

Read this table with care

These rows do not measure the same thing. The paper searches 21M Wikipedia passages and counts a hit when the answer string appears in the passage; we search FiQA passages and count a hit only on a passage someone labelled as an answer. The paper's BM25 was tuned on dev (b = 0.4, k1 = 0.9); ours runs the repo defaults. The only thing worth comparing is the direction and rough size of the gap between BM25 and DPR on each dataset, not the absolute values.

07

Why the first run lost

Not a bug as far as I can tell: the loss goes down and validation recall moves off zero. The recipe was too weak for this data.

Looking back at the run with the papers open, five things stand out. None of them is exotic; they are the settings the DPR paper and the work after it treat as the defaults.

  • Too few negatives. A batch of 16 gives each question 15 other answers to beat, all picked at random. Random finance answers are easy to tell apart from the right one, so most of the batch teaches little. The DPR paper (Karpukhin et al. 2020) uses a batch of 128 plus one BM25 hard negative per question, and RocketQA (Qu et al. 2021) found more negatives across batches and harder negatives to be the main levers for dual encoders.
  • The temperature caps the loss. With normalised vectors and τ = 1 every score sits in [−1, 1], so even a perfect model pays against for a uniform guess (section 02). The run ended its last epoch at a mean of . Models trained on cosine scores use a far smaller τ, 0.05 in SimCSE (Gao et al. 2021) and 0.01 in GTR (Ni et al. 2022), and the DPR paper uses the raw dot product, which has no such ceiling.
  • Too few epochs. Three, where the paper trains for up to 40 epochs on the large datasets and 100 on the small ones.
  • Passages cut at 256 tokens. BEIR truncates documents at 512 word pieces.
  • An old backbone. bert-base-uncased is from 2018. ModernBERT-base (Warner et al. 2024) was trained on far more text, reads 8,192 tokens, and in its paper's single-vector retrieval test (DPR-style training on MS MARCO, scored on BEIR) averages 41.6 nDCG@10 against BERT-base's 38.9.

The second run changes all five at once. That means I cannot say how much each one is worth on its own; a proper ablation is five more runs of two hours each. The temperature is the one I did check separately, in a short run before the long one.

08

The second run

ModernBERT-base, BM25 hard negatives, 128 questions per step, τ = 0.02, 512-token passages, early stopping on validation nDCG@10.

What changed

First and second run from their run.json; paper settings from section 5 of Karpukhin et al. (2020).

Hard negatives from BM25

Every question in a batch is scored against every passage in it, so one step of 128 questions gives each question 1 positive and 255 passages to beat: its own hard negative, the other 127 gold passages and the other 127 hard negatives. FiQA questions often have several gold answers, and one of them can land in the same batch as another question's negative. The loss masks those out of the question's softmax (ContrastiveCollator builds the mask), so a second right answer never counts as a wrong one.

Fitting 128 questions on a 6 GB card

Two trainable ModernBERT towers do not fit: weights, gradients and the two Adam moments are 16 bytes per parameter in fp32, which is about 4.8 GB for 2 × 149M parameters before a single activation. So this run uses one network for both questions and passages (2.4 GB), the setup of GTR and Sentence-BERT rather than the paper's two towers.

The batch itself is handled with a gradient cache (Gao et al. 2021). The step embeds all 128 questions and 256 passages in micro-batches without keeping activations, computes the loss over the full 128 × 256 score matrix, and takes its gradient with respect to the embeddings. Then it embeds one micro-batch at a time again, this time with a graph, and pushes that slice of the gradient through the network. The update is the same as for one big batch (tests/test_trainer.py checks this against plain autograd), but only one micro-batch of activations is in memory at a time. Micro-batches are sorted by length and capped at 16,384 padded tokens, so short passages are not padded to 512. Forward passes run in bf16 autocast with gradient checkpointing on.

Against TF-IDF and BM25

Two BM25 rows

The headline BM25 uses k1 = 0.9, b = 0.4, the Anserini setting behind the BEIR numbers. On our corpus the textbook k1 = 1.5, b = 0.75 from the first run is the stronger of the two, so both are in the table and the dense model has to beat the better one.

Published FiQA numbers are a different test

BEIR (Thakur et al. 2021, Table 2) reports FiQA nDCG@10 of 0.236 for BM25, 0.112 for DPR, 0.295 for ANCE and 0.300 for TAS-B. Those dense models were trained on Natural Questions or MS MARCO and never saw FiQA, and they search all 57,638 FiQA passages. We train on FiQA's own training questions and search passages, every one of them a gold answer to some question. Our numbers are higher across the board for that reason, BM25 included, so only the comparison inside the table above is apples to apples.

09

The first run itself

Hardware, time and the exact configuration, taken from run.json where the run wrote one.

The run was started with this command, plus an output_dir outside the repo:

python train_dpr.py model=hf_encoder train.epochs=3 train.batch_size=16 train.lr=2e-5 device=cuda

Two trainable BERT-base towers only fit in 6 GB at batch 16 because HFEncoder turns on gradient checkpointing. That trades memory for recomputing the forward activations during the backward pass, so every step costs roughly one extra forward pass.
